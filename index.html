Section 01

Biochemical Basics

Where cortisol comes from, how it travels, and what triggers its release.

🧪
Classification
  • Type: Glucocorticoid steroid hormone
  • Origin: Cholesterol (steroidogenesis)
  • Source: Adrenal cortex — Zona Fasciculata
  • Solubility: Lipid-soluble (crosses cell membranes)
  • Receptor: Intracellular glucocorticoid receptor (GR-α)
  • Action: Directly alters gene transcription
🩸
Transport in Blood
  • ~75–80% bound to Corticosteroid-Binding Globulin (CBG/transcortin)
  • ~15% weakly bound to albumin
  • ~5–10% free (biologically active)
  • CBG ↑ in pregnancy, estrogen → more total cortisol (not free)
  • Liver synthesizes CBG; liver disease → ↓ CBG → more free cortisol
⏰
Secretion Pattern
  • Circadian: Peaks 6–8 AM (cortisol awakening response), nadir midnight
  • Pulsatile: 15–20 pulses/day tied to ACTH pulses
  • Light entrains the circadian clock in the SCN
  • Shift work, jet lag → disrupted rhythm
  • Stress overrides circadian pattern
  • Half-life: ~90 min; metabolised in liver
⚡ The fundamental concept: Cortisol is a catabolic, anti-anabolic, anti-inflammatory hormone. In acute stress it is life-saving. Chronically elevated, it is profoundly destructive. Everything below flows from this principle.

🧬
POMC — Pro-opiomelanocortin

ACTH is only one of many peptides cleaved from POMC. Understanding this explains why Addison's disease (↑ACTH) causes hyperpigmentation.

  • ACTH → stimulates cortisol + aldosterone (partially) + adrenal androgens
  • α-MSH (from ACTH) → melanocyte stimulation → skin darkening
  • β-Endorphin → opioid-like analgesia during stress
  • γ-MSH → blood pressure regulation, aldosterone
  • In Addison's: no cortisol → ↑↑ ACTH → ↑ MSH → hyperpigmentation
🔄
Negative Feedback in Detail
  • Fast feedback (seconds–minutes): Cortisol inhibits CRH release via membrane receptors (non-genomic)
  • Delayed feedback (hours): Cortisol enters pituitary nuclei → ↓ POMC transcription → ↓ ACTH synthesis
  • Intermediate: Cortisol ↓ ACTH secretion from stored granules
  • Feedback sites: hypothalamus, pituitary, hippocampus (contextual), prefrontal cortex
  • Dexamethasone (synthetic GC) exploits this for the dexamethasone suppression test
🧪
Dexamethasone Suppression Test
  • Low-dose (1 mg overnight): screens for Cushing's — normal → cortisol suppressed; Cushing's → not suppressed
  • High-dose (8 mg): distinguishes pituitary Cushing's (suppressed) from ectopic ACTH/adrenal (not suppressed)
  • Dex does not cross-react with cortisol immunoassay → pure feedback test
Section 03

Systemic Effects of Cortisol

Cortisol touches nearly every organ system. Here is a complete organ-by-organ breakdown.

⚗️
Metabolic Effects Catabolic
  • Gluconeogenesis ↑: Stimulates hepatic gluconeogenesis via induction of PEPCK, glucose-6-phosphatase; raises blood glucose even in fasted state
  • Glycogen synthesis ↑: In liver only (paradox: also raises glucose by counteracting insulin)
  • Proteolysis ↑: Muscle protein breakdown → amino acids released as gluconeogenic substrates
  • Lipolysis ↑: Stimulates HSL in adipose; mobilises FFAs. But excess causes central/visceral fat deposition (paradox — insulin resistance + hyperphagia)
  • Insulin resistance: Downregulates GLUT4 in muscle, reduces insulin receptor signalling → diabetogenic
  • Appetite ↑: Stimulates hypothalamic NPY and orexigenic pathways; chronic elevation → hyperphagia
  • Glucose utilization ↓ in peripheral tissues
❤️
Cardiovascular Effects Pressor
  • α1-receptor upregulation: Cortisol increases α1-adrenoceptor density on arterioles → greater vasoconstriction to same norepinephrine level
  • Permissive action on catecholamines: Without cortisol, epinephrine and norepinephrine lose much of their vasopressor effect (see Permissive section)
  • Mineralocorticoid spillover: At very high concentrations, cortisol overwhelms 11β-HSD2 and binds aldosterone (mineralocorticoid) receptors → Na⁺ retention → ↑ BP
  • Maintains cardiac output: Required for normal myocardial contractility and vascular tone
  • Addison's crisis: profound hypotension due to loss of all these effects
🦴
Bone & Musculoskeletal
  • Osteoblast activity ↓: Directly inhibits osteoblast proliferation and collagen I synthesis → ↓ bone formation
  • Osteoclast activity ↑ (indirectly): ↓ OPG → ↑ RANKL signalling → ↑ bone resorption
  • Ca²⁺ absorption ↓: Inhibits intestinal calcium absorption (via vitamin D antagonism) → ↑ PTH → bone resorption
  • Renal Ca²⁺ excretion ↑
  • Net: Osteoporosis with chronic excess (hallmark of Cushing's)
  • Muscle: Proteolysis → proximal myopathy (Cushing's sign: cannot rise from chair)
  • Growth plate: Suppresses IGF-1 and GH action → impaired growth in children
🩹
Skin & Connective Tissue
  • Fibroblast activity ↓: Inhibits fibroblast proliferation → poor wound healing
  • Collagen synthesis ↓: Leads to skin thinning, easy bruising
  • Striae: Rapid fat deposition + weakened dermis → purple striae (esp. abdomen, flanks, axillae)
  • Impaired re-epithelialization
  • Decreased keratinocyte proliferation
  • With Cushing's: buffalo hump (dorsocervical fat), moon facies, central obesity
🧠
CNS & Mood Effects
  • Hippocampus: Cortisol receptors dense here; acute → improves memory consolidation; chronic → dendritic atrophy, impairs explicit memory
  • Amygdala: Potentiates fear responses, anxiety; chronic → amygdala hypertrophy
  • Prefrontal cortex: Chronic stress impairs PFC-mediated executive function
  • Mood: Acute → euphoria, heightened alertness; chronic → depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment
  • Sleep: ↑ evening cortisol → insomnia; suppresses REM sleep
  • Psychosis possible with high-dose exogenous glucocorticoids
  • Appetite centers: ↑ NPY, ↑ AgRP in hypothalamus → hyperphagia
🫃
Gastrointestinal
  • ↑ Gastric acid secretion → PUD risk (especially combined with NSAIDs)
  • ↓ Mucus production → impaired gastric mucosal barrier
  • Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis (which normally protects gut mucosa)
  • Can unmask or worsen peptic ulcer disease
  • GI: redistribution of blood flow during stress reduces splanchnic perfusion
💧
Renal / Fluid
  • At physiologic levels: maintains GFR; promotes free water clearance (cortisol required for normal free water excretion — why Addison's patients hyponatremic)
  • Inhibits ADH effect: Cortisol facilitates renal excretion of free water (opposes ADH)
  • At high concentrations: mineralocorticoid receptor binding → Na⁺ retention, K⁺ loss, H₂O retention (see mineralocorticoid section)
  • 11β-HSD2 normally inactivates cortisol → aldosterone receptor; overwhelmed in Cushing's
🧬
Reproductive Axis
  • ↓ GnRH release from hypothalamus → ↓ LH/FSH → ↓ estrogen/testosterone
  • Explains stress-induced amenorrhea, low libido, anovulation
  • ↓ Ovarian sensitivity to gonadotropins
  • Chronic stress → hypothalamic hypogonadism
  • Endometrial effects: impairs implantation
  • Thyroid: cortisol inhibits TSH secretion and peripheral T4→T3 conversion (sick euthyroid pattern)
Section 04

Permissive Effects of Cortisol

Cortisol does not directly produce many effects — instead it "permits" other hormones to act maximally. Without baseline cortisol, those hormones lose much of their potency.

🔑
What "Permissive" Means

A permissive effect is when hormone A (cortisol) does not directly cause an effect, but its presence is required for hormone B to produce its full response. Cortisol upregulates receptors, second messengers, and enzymatic machinery that other hormones rely on.

⚡ Classic teaching point: In adrenal insufficiency, epinephrine injection fails to raise BP adequately until cortisol is replaced. This is why hydrocortisone is given in adrenal crisis.

Mechanism of Permissive Action
Receptor Upregulation
  • Cortisol induces synthesis of receptors for catecholamines (α₁, β₂)
  • More receptors = greater response to same hormone level
  • Takes hours (gene transcription)
Enzyme Induction
  • Induces PEPCK, G6Pase → gluconeogenic machinery ready for glucagon
  • Induces PNMT in adrenal medulla → epinephrine synthesis (cortisol reaches medulla via portal blood)
  • Induces lipase co-factors for lipolysis
Signal Amplification
  • Maintains intracellular cAMP levels by inhibiting phosphodiesterase
  • Preserves G-protein coupling efficiency
  • Prevents receptor desensitization (downregulation) of catecholamine receptors

🧬
11β-HSD2: The Protector Enzyme
  • 11β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 converts cortisol → cortisone (inactive) in kidney
  • Protects aldosterone receptors from cortisol (circulating cortisol is 1000× more abundant than aldosterone)
  • Overwhelmed in: Cushing's syndrome (very high cortisol), licorice ingestion (glycyrrhizin inhibits 11β-HSD2), ectopic ACTH syndrome
  • Genetic deficiency → Apparent Mineralocorticoid Excess (AME) syndrome: hypertension, hypokalemia, low aldosterone
🔄
Renal Tubular Mechanism
  • Principal cells (DCT/CCD): Cortisol (acting as mineralocorticoid) → ↑ ENaC expression (apical) → Na⁺ entry from lumen → ↑ Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (basolateral) → Na⁺ into blood, K⁺ into cell → K⁺ exits via ROMK into lumen → K⁺ secreted
  • Net: Na⁺ retention → volume expansion → ↑ BP → ↓ renin/aldosterone (suppressed)
  • α-Intercalated cells: same MR activation → ↑ H⁺-ATPase → H⁺ secretion → metabolic alkalosis + hypokalemia together = classic electrolyte pattern

Clinical Scenarios
  • Licorice (glycyrrhizin): inhibits 11β-HSD2 → AME picture: HTN, hypokalemia, low aldosterone, low renin
  • Ectopic ACTH (small cell lung cancer): extremely high cortisol → severe hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, HTN — often without cushingoid features (too rapid onset)
  • Cushing's disease: mild-moderate mineralocorticoid effect; ACTH stimulates both cortisol AND aldosterone
  • Primary adrenal Cushing's: aldosterone may be low (ACTH low, and adrenal adenoma mainly makes cortisol)
Section 06

Immune & Inflammatory Effects

Cortisol is the body's most powerful endogenous anti-inflammatory agent. It acts at multiple levels of the immune cascade.

🛡️
Anti-inflammatory Mechanisms
  • Lipocortin/Annexin A1 induction: Inhibits phospholipase A₂ → ↓ arachidonic acid release → ↓ prostaglandins AND ↓ leukotrienes (both pathways blocked)
  • ↓ NF-κB: Cortisol-GR complex directly inhibits NF-κB → ↓ transcription of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, COX-2
  • WBC adhesion molecule ↓: Inhibits ICAM-1, selectin expression → neutrophils cannot extravasate → neutrophilia (cells stuck in blood)
  • Mast cells: Blocks histamine release and mast cell degranulation → less vasodilation, less bronchoconstriction
  • ↓ COX-2: Direct transcriptional suppression → ↓ PGE₂, PGI₂
  • ↓ Cytokine production: Blocks IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, TNF-α
  • ↓ Lymphocyte proliferation: via blocking IL-2 signalling
  • ↑ Apoptosis of lymphocytes and eosinophils
🩸
Changes in White Cell Count (LEANE)
  • Neutrophilia ↑: Cortisol → ↓ adhesion molecules → neutrophils released from marginating pool + ↑ marrow release; demargination is the key mechanism
  • Eosinopenia ↓: Sequestration in spleen + bone marrow + ↑ apoptosis
  • Basopenia ↓: Degranulation inhibited + bone marrow sequestration
  • Lymphopenia ↓: Redistribution to lymphoid organs + ↑ apoptosis (especially T cells); blocks IL-2 → ↓ T-cell proliferation
  • Monocytopenia ↓: Redistribution away from blood
  • Mnemonic LEANE: Lymphocytes, Eosinophils, bAsophils, Neutrophils↑, Erythrocytes (↑ RBC by stimulating EPO marginally)
🦠
Immunosuppression Consequences
  • Reactivation of TB: Latent TB reactivates — cortisol blocks IL-2 (required for Th1 response and macrophage activation to kill Mycobacterium)
  • Candidiasis: Opportunistic fungal infections; ↓ neutrophil killing + ↓ lymphocyte function
  • Herpes zoster reactivation: Impaired T-cell surveillance
  • CMV, PCP: Opportunistic infections especially at high doses
  • Mask signs of infection: No fever, no WBC elevation, no peritoneal signs possible
  • Live vaccines contraindicated in high-dose glucocorticoid therapy
💊
Therapeutic Anti-inflammatory Use
  • Asthma (acute severe): IV hydrocortisone → rapid bronchodilation (permissive for β₂) + anti-inflammatory
  • Anaphylaxis: second-line (epinephrine first) → prevents biphasic reaction
  • Septic shock (controversial): low-dose hydrocortisone in refractory vasoplegic shock
  • Rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, SLE: long-term suppression of autoimmune inflammation
  • Organ transplantation: prevents graft rejection (blocks T-cell IL-2)
  • Neonatal RDS: antenatal betamethasone → fetal lung maturation (induces surfactant)
  • Cerebral edema (tumor): dexamethasone ↓ vasogenic edema
  • Group croup: dexamethasone → ↓ subglottic edema
Section 07

Chronic Stress & HPA Dysregulation

Cortisol is adaptive acutely. Prolonged exposure disrupts every regulatory system in the body.

📈
Stages of Stress Response
Acute (seconds–hours)
Fight-or-Flight Activation
Catecholamines (instant) → cortisol (minutes to hours). Glucose mobilized, immune readied, vascular tone ↑. Adaptive and protective. Negative feedback intact.
Subacute (days–weeks)
Sustained HPA Activation
HPA axis hyperactive. Hippocampal GR receptors begin to downregulate → feedback impaired. Circadian rhythm flattens. Immune function begins to suffer. Sleep disrupted.
Chronic (months–years)
HPA Dysregulation / Cortisol Resistance
GR receptors downregulated systemically → tissues resistant to cortisol → paradox: high cortisol yet pro-inflammatory state. Depression, metabolic syndrome, immunocompromise. Hippocampal atrophy visible on MRI.
Burnout / Exhaustion
Blunted Cortisol Response
In some chronically stressed individuals (PTSD, burnout): low morning cortisol, blunted CAR (cortisol awakening response), flat diurnal pattern. HPA axis "burned out". Associated with fatigue, low resilience.
🧠
Central Mechanisms of HPA Dysregulation
  • Hippocampus: Rich in GR → normally drives negative feedback. Chronic cortisol → dendritic atrophy → loss of inhibitory tone → runaway HPA activity
  • Amygdala: Chronic cortisol → CRF upregulation in amygdala → anxiety, hypervigilance, enhanced fear memory
  • Prefrontal cortex: Dendritic atrophy → poor executive control over stress response
  • Hypothalamus: CRH neurons may become persistently active independent of feedback
  • Glucocorticoid resistance: GR number ↓, affinity ↓, co-activator loss → cells no longer respond normally to anti-inflammatory signal of cortisol → inflammation persists
  • PTSD: often associated with LOW cortisol and enhanced GR sensitivity (opposite pattern)
⚠️
Consequences of Chronic Hypercortisolemia
  • Type 2 diabetes / insulin resistance
  • Central obesity
  • Hypertension
  • Osteoporosis
  • Recurrent infections
  • Depression / anxiety
  • Cognitive decline
  • Impaired wound healing
  • Infertility
  • Cardiovascular disease
🧘
Interventions to Normalize HPA Axis
  • Exercise: Acute cortisol spike but long-term ↓ basal cortisol; improves hippocampal neurogenesis (BDNF)
  • Sleep: Restorative sleep normalizes circadian cortisol pattern
  • Mindfulness/CBT: Reduces amygdala reactivity; improves PFC regulation
  • Social support: Oxytocin opposes cortisol; blunts HPA reactivity
  • Nutrition: Omega-3, adequate protein, low glycemic → reduce inflammatory drive
  • Adaptogens (evidence-limited): Ashwagandha (KSM-66) shown to ↓ salivary cortisol in RCTs
Section 08

Clinical Conditions

Excess and deficiency states, their causes, features, and key distinguishing points.

Cushing's Syndrome Excess Cortisol
Causes
  • Iatrogenic (most common overall): exogenous steroids
  • Cushing's disease: pituitary adenoma → ↑↑ ACTH (most common endogenous)
  • Adrenal adenoma/carcinoma: ↑↑ cortisol, ↓↓ ACTH
  • Ectopic ACTH: SCLC, carcinoid → ↑↑↑ ACTH → ↑↑↑ cortisol
Classic Features
  • Central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump
  • Purple striae (>1 cm)
  • Easy bruising, skin thinning
  • Proximal myopathy
  • Hypertension, hyperglycemia
  • Hypokalemia (esp. ectopic ACTH)
  • Osteoporosis
  • Depression, psychosis
  • Menstrual irregularity, ↓ libido
Diagnostic Approach
  • 24-hr urinary free cortisol (×2)
  • Late-night salivary cortisol (×2)
  • 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test
  • If confirmed: plasma ACTH to distinguish ACTH-dependent vs independent
  • High-dose DST / CRH stimulation / IPSS for pituitary vs ectopic
Addison's Disease Cortisol Deficiency
Causes
  • Autoimmune (most common in developed world): anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies
  • TB (most common worldwide)
  • HIV, CMV adrenalitis
  • Bilateral adrenal metastases
  • Meningococcal sepsis (Waterhouse-Friderichsen)
  • Drugs: ketoconazole, etomidate, rifampicin (↑ metabolism)
Features
  • Fatigue, weight loss, anorexia
  • Hypotension (postural)
  • Hyperpigmentation (↑ ACTH → ↑ MSH)
  • Hyponatremia (↓ free water excretion)
  • Hyperkalemia (↓ aldosterone)
  • Hypoglycemia (↓ gluconeogenesis)
  • Eosinophilia (no cortisol suppression)
  • GI: N/V, abdominal pain
Adrenal Crisis — Emergency
  • Precipitant: infection, surgery, vomiting, trauma
  • Shock, confusion, vomiting, abdominal pain, fever
  • Treatment: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg stat, then 50 mg q6h; IV saline; glucose
  • Do NOT delay treatment for cortisol levels — treat clinically suspected crisis immediately
💊
Exogenous Glucocorticoid Side Effects (dose & duration dependent)
  • HPA axis suppression → adrenal atrophy (never stop abruptly!)
  • Cushing's features (moon face, striae, weight gain)
  • Hyperglycemia / steroid diabetes
  • Dyslipidemia
  • Osteoporosis (prevent with Ca²⁺, Vit D, bisphosphonates)
  • Proximal myopathy
  • Avascular necrosis of femoral head
  • Glaucoma, posterior subcapsular cataracts
  • Growth suppression (children)
  • Immunosuppression: TB, candida, herpes reactivation
  • Skin: thinning, easy bruising, impaired healing
  • Psychiatric: mood elevation, psychosis, insomnia
